One of the most disorienting things about stress-related hair loss is the timing. The difficult period ends, you start to recover, and *then* your hair starts coming out — often two to three months later, when you can no longer see the connection.

That delay is not a coincidence. It is how the hair cycle works.

## The cycle, briefly

Every follicle moves through phases: a long growing phase lasting years, a short transitional phase, and a resting phase of a few months, after which the hair sheds and a new one begins. At any moment most of your hair is growing and a minority is resting, which is why normal shedding goes unnoticed.

A significant stress — physical or psychological — can push an unusually large number of follicles into the resting phase at once. They do not fall out then. They fall out at the end of that resting phase, months afterwards, together.

This pattern has a name, telogen effluvium, and the delay is its defining feature. By the time you notice, the trigger is in the past.

## What cortisol does at the follicle

Cortisol is the body's main stress hormone, and the hair follicle is not a bystander to it.

Researchers have described how sustained cortisol exposure disrupts the hair growth cycle and shortens the growing phase — reviewed by Thom in *Journal of Drugs in Dermatology* ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27538002/)).

More strikingly, a 2021 study in *Nature* by Choi and colleagues identified a specific mechanism by which the stress hormone keeps hair follicle stem cells in a resting state, holding them out of the growth phase ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33790465/)). Chronic stress does not just correlate with hair loss. It acts on the follicle.

## Why this matters for how you think about it

Two things follow.

**First, your body was not malfunctioning.** It was doing something reasonable. Under sustained stress it deprioritises processes it can survive without, and growing hair is high on that list. That is not betrayal. It is triage.

**Second, this is why we treat stress as a root cause rather than a lifestyle footnote.** If cortisol is part of what is driving the loss, then a supplement aimed only at the hair shaft is working downstream of the actual problem. It is also why "just relax" is such an infuriating thing to be told — it names a real factor while offering nothing usable.

## The honest limits

Recovery from stress-related shedding often happens on its own once the trigger has passed, over months. That is genuinely good news, and it means not every case needs intervention.

It also means it is hard to tell what helped. If your hair improves while you are taking something, during a period when it might have improved anyway, you cannot separate the two from the inside. We would rather say that than let you assume otherwise.

And the delay works in both directions: just as the loss showed up months after the trigger, recovery shows up months after things improve.

:::medical
**Not all shedding is stress.**

Thyroid disease, iron deficiency, autoimmune conditions, medication effects, recent illness, and hormonal changes can all produce similar patterns — and some need treatment. If your hair is falling out, get your bloods done, including ferritin and full thyroid function.

If the shedding is sudden, patchy, or accompanied by scalp pain or visible scarring, see a doctor promptly rather than waiting it out.
:::

Sleep is the intervention with the clearest effect on this system, and the one nobody can sell you. It is also, when you are in the middle of a hard period, the hardest thing to fix. We are not going to pretend otherwise.
